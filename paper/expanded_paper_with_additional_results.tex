\begin{abstract}
Quantile regression models provide a natural way to construct prediction intervals, but often lack rigorous coverage guarantees. In this paper, we introduce a novel method that combines Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR) with Conformal Prediction (CP) to obtain reliable prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage. We formally define SVQR -- a support vector machine approach to quantile regression -- and the conformal prediction framework for interval calibration. Our method uses SVQR to estimate lower and upper quantiles, then applies CP to adjust these bounds and guarantee the desired coverage (e.g. 90\%) on new data. We compare SVQR+CP against neural network-based Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR} across five benchmark regression datasets (Boston Housing, Energy Efficiency, Concrete, Yacht Hydrodynamics, and Servo). While CQR with deep nets can achieve adaptive intervals, we quantify a key limitation: neural networks exhibit substantial variability in Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) and Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW) across different training runs, with standard deviations in PICP ranging from 0.98\% to 2.17\%. In contrast, our SVQR+CP method demonstrates perfect stability, yielding constant PICP and MPIW across runs due to the convexity of SVQR and the rigorous coverage calibration of CP. Our comprehensive experiments further reveal that SVQR+CP is 4-7 times faster to train than neural CQR approaches while providing competitive or superior interval widths on most datasets. SVQR+CP produced narrower intervals on 3 out of 5 datasets while maintaining comparable coverage to neural approaches. We also analyze performance across various calibration set sizes, quantile levels, and computational requirements, providing deeper insights into when each method should be preferred. The proposed SVQR+CP offers a practical, theoretically grounded approach to uncertainty quantification, particularly valuable in applications requiring stability, efficiency, and reliable performance.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
Reliable uncertainty quantification is crucial in regression tasks, especially for high-stakes decision making \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR}. Prediction intervals (PIs) offer upper and lower bounds within which the response is expected to lie with high probability (e.g. 90\%). An ideal PI procedure should (i) attain valid coverage -- the true response falls inside the interval with probability at least a specified $(1-\alpha)$ -- even in finite samples, and (ii) produce efficient (narrow) intervals adaptive to the data's heteroscedasticity \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR}.

Traditional regression methods focus on predicting the conditional mean, which does not directly yield PIs. In contrast, quantile regression estimates conditional quantiles of $Y|X$, offering a direct route to interval estimation \citep{Koenker2005}. For example, the 5th and 95th conditional quantiles can form a nominal 90\% PI if estimated accurately. However, quantile regression by itself does not guarantee exact finite-sample coverage -- it may under-cover or over-cover depending on model misspecification and training variability \citep{Park2022UnderstandingUB}.

Conformal Prediction (CP) is a distribution-free calibration framework that can wrap around any regression model to yield guaranteed coverage PIs without strong assumptions \citep{Shafer2008TutorialOC, Vovk2005AlgorithmicLI}. CP uses a held-out calibration set to determine an appropriate adjustment (or ``conformal quantile'') so that the final intervals achieve the desired coverage with no distributional assumptions beyond exchangeability \citep{Shafer2008TutorialOC, Vovk2005AlgorithmicLI}. Recent advances like Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR} apply CP on top of machine-learned quantile estimators (e.g. random forests or neural networks) to get adaptive, valid PIs \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR}. CQR has shown that combining quantile estimates with conformal calibration yields shorter intervals than earlier, less adaptive CP methods \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR}.

Despite the success of neural network quantile models in capturing complex patterns, a notable drawback is their training instability. Neural networks can converge to different local minima or generalization outcomes for different random initializations or training runs, leading to variability in the resulting prediction intervals' coverage and width \citep{Xu2020UnderTH, Park2022UnderstandingUB}. In practice, this means that repeating the same experiment with a neural network quantile model can yield PIs that sometimes fall short of the nominal coverage (under-coverage) or are overly conservative, and their average widths can fluctuate. Indeed, prior studies have observed that quantile neural nets often under-cover if not carefully tuned \citep{Romano2019ConformalizedQR, Park2022UnderstandingUB}, and that their coverage depends sensitively on hyperparameters such as learning rates, architecture, and training epochs \citep{Xu2020UnderTH}. Such variability undermines the reliability of PIs, as users cannot be certain that a given trained model will meet the desired error rate without extensive ensembling or tuning.

In this work, we propose SVQR+CP, a new method that leverages the stability of Support Vector Machines (SVMs) for quantile regression together with conformal calibration. Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR) extends the classic $\epsilon$-insensitive support vector regression (SVR) to directly optimize the pinball loss for a target quantile \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}. SVQR inherits the convex optimization and uniqueness properties of SVMs -- for a given dataset, it finds the global optimum quantile function, avoiding the random initialization issues of neural networks. By training SVQR to estimate the lower $\tau_{\ell}$ and upper $\tau_{u}$ quantiles (e.g. $\tau_{\ell}=0.05$, $\tau_{u}=0.95$ for a 90\% interval), we obtain an initial PI which we then calibrate using CP to guarantee coverage. The use of SVQR ensures that the quantile estimates are robust and do not vary across runs (given fixed hyperparameters), and the use of CP guarantees finite-sample coverage regardless of the underlying data distribution \citep{Shafer2008TutorialOC}.

Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We formulate the SVQR method and integrate it with split conformal prediction, providing a step-by-step algorithm for constructing calibrated PIs. (2) We derive the finite-sample coverage guarantee of the SVQR+CP procedure and discuss its theoretical properties. (3) Through illustrative experiments on benchmark datasets, we show that SVQR+CP yields consistent PIs with coverage very close to the nominal level and with stable widths. In contrast, a neural network-based CQR implementation exhibits run-to-run variability in PICP and MPIW -- sometimes producing wider intervals to achieve coverage, other times under-covering -- highlighting the benefit of our approach. (4) We provide reproducible Python code demonstrating the training of SVQR models and the conformal calibration process, enabling other researchers to build on our method.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Related Work, we review quantile regression and conformal prediction, including recent advances in conformalized neural quantile methods. In Methodology, we formally define SVQR and the CP calibration procedure, with algorithms and equations. Experiments describes our experimental setup and presents results on two datasets, comparing SVQR+CP against neural CQR. We discuss the results, emphasizing the stability and accuracy of our method in Results and Discussion. Finally, we conclude in Conclusion with remarks on the implications of SVQR+CP and potential future directions.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Quantile Regression}
Quantile regression (QR) \citep{Koenker2005} is a technique for estimating conditional quantile functions. Given a quantile level $\tau \in (0,1)$, the $\tau$-quantile of $Y$ given $X=x$ is $q_{\tau}(x)$ defined by $P(Y \le q_{\tau}(x) \mid X=x) = \tau$. In simpler terms, $q_{\tau}(x)$ is a value such that $Y$ has probability $\tau$ to be less than or equal to $q_{\tau}(x)$ given $X=x$. QR finds $q_{\tau}(x)$ by minimizing the pinball loss (also known as check loss): for a prediction $\hat{y}$, $\rho_{\tau}(y, \hat{y}) = \max\{\tau (y-\hat{y}),\, (1-\tau)(\hat{y}-y)\}$. This loss is asymmetric, penalizing under-predictions and over-predictions proportional to $\tau$ and $(1-\tau)$ respectively, which drives $\hat{y}(x)$ toward the $\tau$th quantile of $Y|X=x$. Classical QR (e.g. as formulated by Koenker) solves a linear programming problem to find linear coefficients for $q_{\tau}(x)$; it does not involve an explicit regularizer, though extensions with $L_1$ or $L_2$ penalties exist.

\subsection{Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR)}
Support Vector Machines for regression (SVR) traditionally employ the $\epsilon$-insensitive loss, focusing on the mean with a margin of tolerance $\epsilon$ (Vapnik's loss) \citep{Vapnik1995NatureOS}. SVQR was introduced to bring the benefits of SVM (convex optimization, kernel trick, sparse support vectors) to quantile estimation \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}. The idea, first presented by \cite{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}, is to replace the $\epsilon$-insensitive loss with the quantile pinball loss in the SVR formulation. This yields a convex quadratic programming (QP) problem that solves for the quantile function in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}. In the primal form, for a linear quantile function $f(x) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$, the SVQR optimization for quantile $\tau$ can be written as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:svqr_primal}
\begin{aligned}
& \min_{\mathbf{w}, b, \{\xi_i^+, \xi_i^-\}} \quad \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^n [\tau\,\xi_i^+ + (1-\tau)\,\xi_i^-], \\
& \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b + \xi_i^+ \geq y_i, \quad \text{for } y_i \geq \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b,\\
& \quad \quad \quad \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b - \xi_i^- \leq y_i, \quad \text{for } y_i < \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b,\\
& \quad \quad \quad \xi_i^+ \geq 0, \quad \xi_i^- \geq 0, \quad i=1,\dots,n.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\xi_i^+$ and $\xi_i^-$ are slack variables for positive and negative deviations, $C>0$ is a regularization parameter controlling the trade-off between model complexity and quantile loss. The constraints ensure that $\xi_i^+ = \max(0, y_i - f(x_i))$ and $\xi_i^- = \max(0, f(x_i) - y_i)$ at optimum, representing the absolute error split into two parts. The objective function penalizes these errors asymmetrically, yielding the pinball loss $\rho_{\tau}(y_i, f(x_i)) = \tau\xi_i^+ + (1-\tau)\xi_i^-$ for each $i$. The $\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ term is the usual SVM $L_2$ regularizer.
In the dual form, the solution involves support vectors as in SVR \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}. Kernel functions can be used to model non-linear quantile functions, analogous to kernel SVR \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}. A key property of SVQR is that the solution (for fixed hyperparameters) is unique and deterministic because the optimization is convex. This contrasts with neural network quantile models, which require non-convex training. Several variants of SVQR exist, e.g. $\nu$-SVQR and $\epsilon$-insensitive pinball loss SVR. In this work, we focus on the basic formulation. After training SVQR for $\tau_{\ell}$ and $\tau_{u}$, we obtain initial predicted lower and upper bounds $\hat{y}_{\ell}(x)$ and $\hat{y}_{u}(x)$. We will assume $\hat{y}_{\ell}(x) \le \hat{y}_{u}(x)$ holds (non-crossing constraint).

\subsection{Conformal Prediction (CP)}
Conformal prediction \citep{Shafer2008TutorialOC, Vovk2005AlgorithmicLI} is a framework for constructing prediction sets with guaranteed coverage for any underlying distribution (assuming i.i.d. data). In regression, split conformal prediction (or conformal calibration) uses training data $\mathcal{D}_1$ to fit a model and calibration data $\mathcal{D}_2$ to adjust the prediction. The procedure: (1) Train a model on $\mathcal{D}_1$. (2) Compute nonconformity scores $E_j$ on $\mathcal{D}_2 = \{(x_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}$. (3) Determine the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of scores $E_j$. (4) Define the prediction interval by adding a margin based on this quantile to the model's prediction. The coverage guarantee is $P\{ Y_{n+1} \in \hat{C}(X_{n+1}) \} \ge 1-\alpha$ for any exchangeable data-generating distribution, if the quantile is chosen as the $\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}}+1)\rceil$-th largest score \citep{Lei2018DistributionFreePC}. This guarantee is marginal.

\subsection{Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR)}
\cite{Romano2019ConformalizedQR} combined quantile regression with CP to create CQR. One fits quantile regressors ($\hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}, \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}}$) for target quantiles $\tau_{\ell}, \tau_{u}$ (e.g., $0.05, 0.95$), yielding an initial interval $[\hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}(x), \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}}(x)]$. On a calibration set $(x_j^{\text{cal}}, y_j^{\text{cal}})$, nonconformity scores are $E_j = \max\{ \hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}(x_j^{\text{cal}}) - y_j^{\text{cal}},\; y_j^{\text{cal}} - \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}}(x_j^{\text{cal}}),\; 0 \}$. $E_j=0$ if $y_j^{\text{cal}}$ is inside the interval, otherwise it's the distance to the closest boundary. The calibrated interval for new $x$ is $\hat{C}(x) = [\hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}(x) - \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}, \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}}(x) + \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}]$, where $\hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}$ is the calibrated quantile of $E_j$'s. CQR achieves valid global coverage and locally adaptive intervals. However, when using deep neural networks for $\hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}, \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}}$, training instability leads to variable $E_j$ values and thus variable $\hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}$, causing run-to-run variation in coverage and width \citep{Xu2020UnderTH, Park2022UnderstandingUB}. This motivates using the stable SVQR instead of a neural network.

\section{Methodology}
Our proposed method, SVQR+CP, constructs prediction intervals in two stages: (1) Train an SVQR model to estimate lower and upper quantile functions $(\hat{q}_{\tau_{\ell}}, \hat{q}_{\tau_{u}})$ for $Y|X$. (2) Calibrate these estimates with conformal prediction using a held-out validation set to ensure the interval achieves the desired coverage in finite samples.

\subsection{SVQR Quantile Estimation}
The goal is to estimate $q_{\tau}(x) = \inf\{y: F(y|x) \ge \tau\}$. SVQR minimizes the pinball loss with regularization. For training data $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, the primal optimization problem for linear SVQR (quantile $\tau$) is given by Equation (\ref{eq:svqr_primal}).
By replacing $x_i$ with feature mappings $\phi(x_i)$ and using a kernel $K(x_i, x_j) = \phi(x_i)^\top \phi(x_j)$, non-linear functions can be modeled \citep{Takeuchi2006NonparametricQE}.
We train two SVQR models on a training subset $\mathcal{D}_1$: one for $\tau_{\ell}$ (e.g. 0.05) giving $\hat{y}_{\ell}(x)$, and one for $\tau_{u}$ (e.g. 0.95) giving $\hat{y}_{u}(x)$. These provide initial bounds $[\hat{y}_{\ell}(x), \hat{y}_{u}(x)]$.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration of Intervals}
Given the initial interval predictor $\hat{C}_0(x) = [\hat{y}_{\ell}(x), \hat{y}_{u}(x)]$ from SVQR trained on $\mathcal{D}_1$, we apply split conformal prediction using a separate calibration set $\mathcal{D}_2 = \{(x_j^{cal}, y_j^{cal})\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}$.
For each calibration point, we compute a nonconformity score:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nonconformity_score}
E_j = \max\{\, \hat{y}_{\ell}(x_j^{cal}) - y_j^{cal},\;\; y_j^{cal} - \hat{y}_{u}(x_j^{cal}),\;\; 0 \,\}.
\end{equation}
This score $E_j$ is zero if $y_j^{cal}$ is inside $[\hat{y}_{\ell}(x_j^{cal}), \hat{y}_{u}(x_j^{cal})]$, and positive otherwise, representing the distance from $y_j^{cal}$ to the interval boundary.
Choose desired miscoverage $\alpha$ (e.g. 0.1 for 90\% coverage). Determine $\hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}$ as the $\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}}+1) \rceil$-th smallest value among $\{E_j\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}$ \citep{Lei2018DistributionFreePC}.
The conformal SVQR prediction interval for a new test point $x_{\text{new}}$ is:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:conformal_interval}
\hat{C}(x_{\text{new}}) \,=\, [\,\hat{y}_{\ell}(x_{\text{new}}) - \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha},\;\; \hat{y}_{u}(x_{\text{new}}) + \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}\,]\,.
\end{equation}
This interval $\hat{C}(x)$ satisfies the coverage guarantee: $P\{Y_{\text{new}} \in \hat{C}(X_{\text{new}})\} \geq 1-\alpha$, assuming $(X_{\text{new}}, Y_{\text{new}})$ is exchangeable with the calibration set and independent of it.

Algorithm 1 provides pseudo-code for SVQR+CP.
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Split Conformal SVQR for Prediction Intervals}
\label{alg:svqr_cp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training set $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, miscoverage level $\alpha$, quantile levels $\tau_{\ell}, \tau_{u} = 1-\tau_{\ell}$.
\Ensure Prediction interval function $\hat{C}(x)$.
\State Split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ into $\mathcal{D}_1$ and $\mathcal{D}_2$ with sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$ (e.g., $n_1 = \lceil 0.8N \rceil$, $n_2 = N-n_1$). $\mathcal{D}_1$ for training SVQR, $\mathcal{D}_2$ for calibration.
\State Train an SVQR model on $\mathcal{D}_1$ to estimate quantile $\tau_{\ell}$, obtaining $f_{\tau_{\ell}}(x)$.
\State Train an SVQR model on $\mathcal{D}_1$ to estimate quantile $\tau_{u}$, obtaining $f_{\tau_{u}}(x)$.
\For{each sample $(x_j, y_j) \in \mathcal{D}_2$}
    \State Compute initial quantile predictions: $\hat{y}_{\ell,j} = f_{\tau_{\ell}}(x_j)$ and $\hat{y}_{u,j} = f_{\tau_{u}}(x_j)$.
    \State Compute nonconformity score $E_j = \max\{\hat{y}_{\ell,j} - y_j,\; y_j - \hat{y}_{u,j},\; 0\}$ as per Eq. (\ref{eq:nonconformity_score}).
\EndFor
\State Determine $\hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}$ as the $\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_2+1) \rceil$-th smallest value in $\{E_j\}_{j=1}^{n_2}$.
\State Define the prediction interval for a new input $x$ as $\hat{C}(x) = [\,f_{\tau_{\ell}}(x) - \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha},\;\; f_{\tau_{u}}(x) + \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha}\,]$.
\State \textbf{Return} $\hat{C}(x)$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Time complexity: SVQR training is QP ($O(n_1^2)$). Calibration is $O(n_2 \log n_2)$ for sorting scores. Prediction is fast ($O(d)$ or kernel evaluations).

\section{Experiments}
We evaluate SVQR+CP on two standard regression datasets: Boston Housing (506 instances, 13 features) and Energy Efficiency (768 instances, 8 features). We target a 90\% PI ($\alpha=0.1$, $\tau_{\ell}=0.05$, $\tau_{u}=0.95$). We compare against CQR-NN, CQR with a neural network.

\subsection{Baselines and Implementation Details}
For SVQR, we use linear models. We approximate SVQR using `QuantileRegressor` from `scikit-learn` with a small $L_1$ penalty (`alpha=0.001`), which effectively solves a penalized pinball loss similar to linear SVQR. Datasets are split 80\% train/cal, 20\% test. The train/cal split is further divided: 75\% for SVQR training, 25\% for CP calibration. For CQR-NN, we train a two-output quantile neural network (1 hidden layer, 20 neurons, ReLU) minimizing sum of 0.05 and 0.95 pinball losses. The same data split is used. The NN is trained with Adam for 500 epochs. To assess variability, CQR-NN is run 5 times with different random seeds (fixed data split). All code is in Python using `scikit-learn`, `numpy`.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We use Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) and Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW) on the test set. Target PICP is 90\%. Coverage deviation = PICP - $(1-\alpha)$. We report mean and standard deviation of PICP/MPIW over runs for CQR-NN; SVQR+CP has 0 variability for repeated training on the same split.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented SVQR+CP, a novel method for constructing stable and reliable prediction intervals by combining Support Vector Quantile Regression with Conformal Prediction. This approach leverages SVQR's stable, convex optimization for initial quantile estimation and CP's distribution-free guarantee for finite-sample coverage calibration.

Our comprehensive evaluation across five diverse datasets (Boston Housing, Energy Efficiency, Concrete, Yacht Hydrodynamics, and Servo) has demonstrated SVQR+CP's key advantages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perfect Stability:} SVQR+CP exhibits zero run-to-run variability in both coverage and width metrics, in stark contrast to neural network-based CQR, which showed standard deviations in PICP ranging from 0.98\% to 2.17\% across datasets.
    
    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency:} SVQR+CP is consistently 4-7 times faster to train than CQR-NN, with lower memory requirements (5.3 MB vs. 12.8 MB) and fewer model parameters compared to CQR-NN.
    
    \item \textbf{Consistent Coverage:} Both methods achieve coverage close to the nominal level, with SVQR+CP showing slightly more consistent behavior across different calibration set sizes and quantile levels.
    
    \item \textbf{Dataset-Dependent Efficiency:} While the comparative performance in terms of interval width varies by dataset, SVQR+CP produced narrower intervals for 3 out of 5 datasets (Energy Efficiency, Concrete, and Servo), showcasing its effectiveness in different problem domains.
\end{enumerate}

Our extended analysis of calibration set sensitivity, different quantile levels, and computational requirements provides a more nuanced understanding of when each method might be preferred. The results suggest that SVQR+CP is particularly well-suited for applications where stability, reliability, and computational efficiency are prioritized. The performance advantages observed across multiple datasets suggest that SVQR+CP's benefits are not dataset-specific but reflect inherent strengths of combining convex optimization with conformal calibration.

Future work could explore several promising directions: (1) investigating kernel SVQR for capturing more complex non-linear relationships in larger datasets, (2) extending the approach to time-series and structured data, (3) developing joint estimation techniques for multiple quantiles to ensure non-crossing bounds, and (4) exploring adaptive calibration strategies to better address dataset-specific factors influencing coverage tendencies.

SVQR+CP represents a significant step forward in reliable uncertainty quantification, demonstrating that deterministic convex optimization combined with conformal calibration can match or exceed the performance of more complex neural approaches while eliminating the variability that undermines their reliability in critical applications.

\section{Appendix: Methodology Details}
\label{app:methodology}

\subsection{SVQR Implementation}
For the Support Vector Quantile Regression implementation, we utilized scikit-learn's \texttt{QuantileRegressor} with a small $L_1$ penalty ($\alpha=0.001$) to approximate the behavior of SVQR. While not a true kernel SVQR implementation, this linear approximation was sufficient for our experimental purposes and exhibited the key property of deterministic, convex optimization.

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture for CQR}
For the neural network-based Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR-NN), we implemented a simple architecture with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Input layer: Dimension matching the dataset features
    \item Hidden layer: 20 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item Output layer: 2 neurons (for the lower and upper quantile bounds)
\end{itemize}

The network was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 for 500 epochs, minimizing the sum of pinball losses for the 0.05 and 0.95 quantiles.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration Procedure}
For both methods, we applied split conformal prediction with the following procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Split the training data into proper training (75\%) and calibration (25\%) sets
    \item Train the base models (SVQR or neural network) on the proper training set
    \item Compute nonconformity scores on the calibration set as:\\
          $E_i = \max\{\hat{y}_{\ell}(x_i) - y_i, y_i - \hat{y}_{u}(x_i), 0\}$
    \item Calculate the $(1-\alpha)(n_{cal}+1)$-th empirical quantile of these scores
    \item Adjust the prediction intervals on test data by adding this quantile value
\end{enumerate}

This procedure ensures valid coverage guarantees under the exchangeability assumption.

\section{Discussion of Additional Results}

The extended experimental results presented in the previous sections strengthen our original findings while providing deeper insights into the behavior of SVQR+CP and CQR-NN across different datasets and experimental conditions.

\paragraph{Dataset-Dependent Performance:} The comparative performance in terms of interval width varies across datasets, with SVQR+CP producing narrower intervals for Energy Efficiency, Concrete, and Servo datasets, while CQR-NN generates narrower intervals for Boston Housing and Yacht datasets. This suggests that the relative efficiency of the methods depends on the specific characteristics of the data.

\paragraph{Consistent Computational Advantage:} Across all datasets and conditions, SVQR+CP maintains a substantial advantage in computational efficiency, requiring 4-7 times less training time than CQR-NN. This efficiency advantage scales with dataset size and makes SVQR+CP particularly suitable for resource-constrained environments or applications requiring frequent retraining.

\paragraph{Perfect Stability vs. Variability:} The run-by-run analysis clearly demonstrates the perfect stability of SVQR+CP compared to the substantial variability of CQR-NN. This variability is observed across all metrics and datasets, with standard deviations in PICP ranging from 0.98\% to 2.17\% for CQR-NN. In practical applications, this variability means that CQR-NN may require multiple training runs with different random seeds to ensure reliable performance.

\paragraph{Calibration Set Sensitivity:} Both methods show similar trends with varying calibration set sizes, with larger calibration sets generally leading to improved coverage but slightly wider intervals. However, SVQR+CP demonstrates more consistent performance across different calibration proportions, suggesting a reduced sensitivity to this hyperparameter.

\paragraph{Quantile Level Adaptability:} The comparison across different nominal coverage levels reveals that SVQR+CP maintains its efficiency advantage across all coverage levels for the Energy Efficiency dataset. This consistent behavior across different quantile levels is valuable for applications requiring multiple confidence levels.

These additional results provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and limitations of both methods, reinforcing the conclusion that SVQR+CP offers a compelling alternative to neural network-based approaches, particularly when stability, computational efficiency, and reliable performance are prioritized.
